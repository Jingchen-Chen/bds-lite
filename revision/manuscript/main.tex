\title{When does boundary distillation help lightweight medical image segmentation?}

\author[1]{Jingchen Chen}
\author[1]{Chenxiang Hong}
\author[1]{Yao Wu}
\author[1]{Tao Yang}
\affil[1]{Future Technology School, Shenzhen Technology University, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China}
\corrauthor[1]{Tao Yang}{yangtao@sztu.edu.cn}

\begin{abstract}
Boundary errors matter whenever a segmentation is used to measure an outline, and training-only boundary signals promise sharper contours from a compact network at no inference cost. We asked when such signals help a compact 2D U-Net beyond what an auxiliary boundary decoder (D) already provides. Each distillation design, including the in-network feature distillation and gate of our Boundary-Distilled Segmentation Lite (BDS-Lite) recipe, was compared with the same network trained with D alone, and every recipe was retrained several times (\R{FIN.runs.total} runs in total) on ISIC2018, ACDC and Synapse. The standard deviation of Boundary F1 between retrainings of one recipe (up to \R{RV.sd.isic2018.max} in the ten-run ISIC2018 arms) was as large as the effects under study on the same dataset. In-network distillation added no detectable gain beyond D (three runs per arm on each dataset), and the complete recipe scored lower in Boundary F1 than D alone on ISIC2018 (\R{TEN.full.decoder.boundary_f1.delta}, 95\% CI \R{TEN.full.decoder.boundary_f1.text}; confirmed on fresh seeds). Distillation from a frozen external teacher raised Boundary F1 by \R{CF.delta} (\R{FIN.cf.text}; \R{CF.n} runs per arm) on \R{OT.patients} held-out ACDC patients, in a confirmatory study whose analysis plan was fixed before the test patients were used; because the unrounded lower bound, \R{RAW.FIN.cf.low}, lies just below the preset smallest effect of interest, 0.01, the prespecified rule rates this a gain of unclear size. Gains also appeared in exploratory controls with whole-image distillation and with a compact teacher that scored below D, consistent with a regularizing effect. On the same ACDC patients, flip-and-rotation augmentation of D raised Boundary F1 several times more than distillation did (exploratory, not prespecified), and with augmentation in both arms no distillation gain was detected. On ACDC, the augmentation regime mattered more than the choice of boundary signal, and effects of this size could be resolved only over repeated training runs.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

\emph{Motivation.} Segmentation is used to measure the outline of a structure as much as its area. In dermoscopy, border irregularity enters the assessment of melanoma \citep{Nachbar1994ABCD}; in cardiac MRI, the contours of the ventricles and myocardium determine ventricular volumes and myocardial mass \citep{Bernard2018Deep}; in abdominal CT, organ outlines are drawn for radiotherapy planning \citep{Jabbour2014Upper}. In each case a displaced contour changes the measurement even when most of the region is labelled correctly. Overlap scores register such errors unevenly, because the same contour shift costs a small structure far more overlap than a large one (Materials \& Methods). Boundary F1, the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) \citep{Huttenlocher1993Comparing} and the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) measure contour error directly, and evaluation guidance recommends reporting them with overlap \citep{Taha2015Metrics, Maier-Hein2024Metrics}.

\emph{Why a compact U-Net.} Segmentation networks have grown through attention gates, transformer encoders and self-configuring pipelines \citep{Oktay2018Attention, Chen2021TransUNet, Cao2022SwinUnet, Isensee2021nnUNet}. Recent work on tumours follows the same direction; for liver tumours in CT, for example, a vision-transformer classifier that flags slices containing tumour has been paired with a Swin-transformer U-Net that segments it \citep{Raj2026ViTSwinUNet}. Such models gain accuracy at the cost of memory and latency, and compact U-Nets remain common where both are limited \citep{Ruan2023EGEUNet, Jiang2024LVUNet}. Because a compact network has little spare capacity, training-only signals that leave the inference network unchanged are particularly attractive there. The choice of a compact model also follows from the size of the effects. Differences in Boundary F1 between boundary signals are of the order of the variation between retrainings, and resolving them took 10 to \R{CF.n} training runs per arm; we therefore spent the compute budget on repeated runs rather than on model size. Holding the architecture fixed also lets a difference between arms be assigned to the training signal.

\emph{Background.} Training-time boundary signals take three forms: losses on contour error, auxiliary boundary branches that are discarded after training, and distillation, which moves the knowledge of a boundary model into the inference network. BDS-Lite, a recipe that we designed for compact U-Nets, combines them: an auxiliary boundary decoder (D), a surface loss (S), feature distillation from the boundary decoder into the segmentation decoder (F) and a gate driven by the distilled feature (G). Each component follows a published pattern (Related work). Distillation can also come from outside the network, as class probabilities of a separately trained teacher \citep{Hinton2015Distilling, Liu2024BPKD}.

\emph{Research gaps.} What is known about boundary distillation is limited by how it has been tested. A boundary branch that shares an encoder with the segmentation path already shapes the shared features, so distillation should be credited only with what it adds to the same network trained with the branch and no transfer. Effects as small as the variation between retrainings of one recipe cannot be shown by one training per arm or by a test over the images of one trained model. And a distillation design usually changes where the loss acts and what the teacher knows at the same time, so a gain can be located only by controls that change one of these.

\emph{Contributions.} We ask when a training-only boundary signal, and distillation in particular, improves contour agreement in a compact U-Net beyond what an auxiliary boundary decoder already provides. Every distillation design is compared with the network trained with the boundary decoder alone (D), every arm is retrained several times, and all models are evaluated on held-out test data. The study provides (1) a controlled component ablation of BDS-Lite on three datasets, extended to ten runs per arm on ISIC2018 with a decision on fresh seeds; (2) a confirmatory replication of band-limited distillation from an external teacher on held-out ACDC patients, with an analysis plan fixed and timestamped before the test patients were used; (3) controls that remove the band restriction and the teacher's advantage in turn, and a comparison of the same distillation under augmentation; and (4) run-to-run standard deviations of Boundary F1 that can serve as a noise baseline when planning studies of this kind.

\section*{Related work}

\emph{Boundary losses.} Boundary loss integrates softmax probabilities against a signed distance map and is scheduled against a region loss \citep{Kervadec2019Boundary}. Hausdorff-oriented losses penalize large surface deviations at the cost of heavier distance computation \citep{Karimi2020Reducing}. Generalized Surface Loss (GSL) normalizes a distance-weighted error and anneals from a region term to a surface term \citep{Celaya2024A}, and Boundary DoU and active boundary loss act in a band around, or along, the contour \citep{Sun2023Boundary, Wang2022Active}. None adds inference cost; their reported gains come from different backbones and protocols, and the backbone can matter within one study: on whole-heart segmentation, a Boundary DoU term added to binary cross-entropy helped a convolutional U-Net under adaptive weighting, whereas a larger boundary weight did not help the transformer-based SwinUNETR \citep{Sutassananon2025Adaptive}.

\emph{Boundary branches.} Psi-Net predicts mask, contour and distance map jointly \citep{Murugesan2019PsiNet}, Gated-SCNN routes shape information through a gated stream \citep{Takikawa2019GatedSCNN}, LB-UNet keeps boundary modules in a lightweight lesion network \citep{Xu2024LBUNet}, and CTO guides its decoder with boundary masks from fixed edge-detection operators in a network with a convolutional and a vision-transformer encoder stream \citep{Lin2025Rethinking}; these pathways stay at inference. STDC discards after training a detail head trained with binary cross-entropy and Dice \citep{Fan2021STDC}, which is the pattern of our decoder-only arm.

\emph{Distillation.} Knowledge distillation trains a student on a teacher's soft outputs \citep{Hinton2015Distilling}. Feature distillation matches intermediate representations through a learned regressor because student and teacher shapes differ \citep{Romero2015FitNets}. In segmentation, distillation has transferred structured outputs \citep{Liu2019Structured} and boundary and body knowledge from a separate teacher \citep{Liu2024BPKD}, and self-distillation within one network has been applied to 3D medical segmentation \citep{Wang2023MISSU, Banerjee2024Dual}. A student can also gain from a teacher no larger than itself \citep{Furlanello2018Born}; in medical segmentation, a teacher of the student's own architecture, trained on class-wise averaged images, has been used to pass a shape and intensity prior to the student \citep{Dong2025Shape}. Soft targets have been analysed as a learned form of label-smoothing regularization, under which even a poorly trained teacher or a hand-designed target distribution can improve a student \citep{Yuan2020Revisiting}.

\emph{Measuring small effects.} Objectives of a multitask recipe can interfere in a shared encoder \citep{Yu2020PCGrad}, so a recipe needs ablation before any part is credited. Rank-based tests over test samples are recommended for comparing fixed models \citep{Demsar2006Statistical}; comparing training recipes also requires the variation between training runs.

Because the methods differ in what they keep at inference and in the backbone and protocol of their original evaluation, we compare boundary signals on one fixed backbone (Supplementary S1 also lists the metric families each source reports).

\section*{Materials \& Methods}

\subsection*{Study design}
The objects of comparison are training recipes: every arm was trained several times with different seeds, and the training run is the unit of replication. The work comprises five studies (Table~\ref{tab:studies}): a matched ablation with reference methods on three datasets; a ten-run extension on ISIC2018; a first study of external-teacher distillation on the three datasets; a replication of that design on held-out ACDC patients; and controls and an augmentation comparison on the same patients. Two studies are confirmatory, because their decision rule was fixed before the deciding data were used: the ten-run extension decides on seeds that had not been trained when the rule was set, and the replication's primary contrast, estimator and decision rule were fixed, and timestamped, before its test patients were first used (Supplementary S18). The other studies are exploratory. The design comprises \R{FIN.runs.total} training runs, including four teachers.

\emph{Relation to the first version.} An earlier version of this study evaluated ISIC2018 on \R{RV.sub.isic2018.n.all} images of which \R{RV.sub.isic2018.n.train} were training images of the evaluated models, scored a class absent from both prediction and reference as 1, and tested differences over the images of single models; its conclusion that BDS-Lite helped on the single-contour task rested on that evaluation and is withdrawn. All three points were corrected (models are evaluated on the \R{DS.isic2018.test.n} official ISIC2018 test images, empty pairs are excluded, and the training run is the unit of analysis), and every arm was retrained with the present code (Supplementary S4, S5).

\subsection*{Framework: contour error, overlap, and the routes of a boundary signal}
\emph{Contour displacement and overlap.} If each point of a contour of length $L$ around an object of area $A$ is displaced along the normal by an offset $\delta$, and $\overline{|\delta|}$ is the mean absolute offset along the contour, the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) falls to first order by
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{DSC}\approx\frac{L\,\overline{|\delta|}}{2A},
\label{eq:dsc}
\end{equation}
whereas ASSD is about $\overline{|\delta|}$. The absolute value matters: outward and inward displacements both add to the disagreement between the two masks and do not cancel. The sensitivity of DSC to contour error therefore depends on the perimeter-to-area ratio $L/A$ and varies between structures: a one-pixel shift costs about \R{S.isic2018.1.drop} DSC on an average ISIC2018 skin lesion but about \R{S.acdc.2.drop} on the ACDC myocardium (Supplementary S3). For this reason contour error is measured directly, with Boundary F1 as the primary endpoint, and scores are not compared across datasets.

\emph{Routes of a boundary signal.} In a network trained with an auxiliary boundary decoder, a boundary signal can reach the segmentation output through the encoder that the two decoders share, through a distillation loss that pulls a segmentation feature towards the boundary decoder's feature, and through a gate that lets the distilled feature rescale the feature from which the prediction is made. A fourth route lies outside the network: a loss on the output from the class probabilities of a separate teacher, restricted to a band around the teacher's contours or applied to the whole image. Arm D uses only the first route. It is the reference for every distillation design, so a difference from D measures what the distillation term adds.

\subsection*{Arms}
Every arm is a four-level 2D U-Net \citep{Ronneberger2015UNet} with base width 32 (\R{PAR.unet.m}\,M parameters on ISIC2018) trained with a subset of four components (Table~\ref{tab:arms}, Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}): a boundary decoder (D), a surface loss (S), feature distillation (F) and a gate (G). U is the plain U-Net. BDS-Lite is the complete recipe D+F+G+S, and its in-network transfer is the addition of F and G to D. External-teacher distillation adds a loss from a separate, frozen teacher to D: D+KD-band and its two controls, D+KD-full (whole image) and D+KD-band$_{\mathrm{cmp}}$ (a compact teacher of the student's own architecture). The remaining ablation cells (U+S, D+S, D+G+S, D+F+S) complete the component design.

Four reference methods were trained under the same protocol. Three are reported in the main text, chosen to cover the alternatives to an auxiliary branch: two surface losses that add no inference cost, U-Net with Generalized Surface Loss (U-Net+GSL) \citep{Celaya2024A} and U-Net with Boundary Loss \citep{Kervadec2019Boundary}, and one architectural change of similar size, Attention U-Net \citep{Oktay2018Attention} (implementations: Supplementary S3). The surface-loss methods change the boundary signal itself, so they are compared with U and D and are not used as the reference for distillation. The fourth, an adaptation of the lightweight boundary network LB-UNet \citep{Xu2024LBUNet} to multiclass output, is not an exact reproduction of the published network and is reported in Supplementary S7.

\subsection*{Data}
Three public benchmarks were used. They were chosen because their structures differ in number, size and perimeter-to-area ratio, the quantities that set how strongly overlap responds to a contour shift (Eq.~\ref{eq:dsc}): one lesion per image, three nested cardiac structures, and eight abdominal organs of very different size. The three tasks were not selected to form a scale of difficulty. ISIC2018 Task~1 \citep{Codella2019Skin, Tschandl2018HAM10000}, from the ISIC Challenge archive (\url{https://challenge.isic-archive.com/data/}), provides dermoscopic images with one lesion class; in \R{CMP.isic2018.single}\% of training masks the lesion is a single component. ACDC \citep{Bernard2018Deep}, from the Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (\url{https://www.creatis.insa-lyon.fr/Challenge/acdc/}), provides cine MRI with right ventricle (RV), myocardium and left ventricle (LV). Synapse \citep{Landman2015MICCAI}, the multi-atlas abdominal CT set (doi:10.7303/syn3193805), is used with eight organs. The tasks differ in how often a structure is absent from a slice; they also differ in modality and sample size.

\emph{Splits.} The ISIC2018 Task~1 training set was divided into \R{SPL.isic2018.train} training and \R{SPL.isic2018.val} validation images, and models are evaluated on the \R{DS.isic2018.test.n} official test images, which share no image identifier with the training and validation images (Supplementary S4). The 100 ACDC training patients are split by patient into \R{DS.acdc.train.units} training, \R{DS.acdc.val.units} validation and \R{DS.acdc.test.units} internal test patients (\R{SPL.acdc.train}, \R{SPL.acdc.val} and \R{SPL.acdc.test} slices); from the replication on, the \R{OT.patients} official test patients 101--150 (\R{OT.slices} slices) are the test set. Synapse is split by case into \R{DS.synapse.train.units} training, \R{DS.synapse.val.units} validation and \R{DS.synapse.test.units} test cases (\R{SPL.synapse.train}, \R{SPL.synapse.val} and \R{SPL.synapse.test} slices); the test cases are the standard TransUNet test cases \citep{Chen2021TransUNetArxiv}, and we train on 14 rather than 18 cases because four are held out for validation.

\emph{Preprocessing.} ACDC and Synapse volumes are processed as 2D slices. Inputs are $224\times224$ arrays, resized with continuous interpolation for images and nearest-neighbour interpolation for masks; ACDC is stored per-volume z-scored, Synapse CT is windowed to $[0,1]$, and ISIC2018 is RGB. The ACDC training data are a third-party conversion of the original volumes; the official ACDC test patients were preprocessed with our reconstruction of that conversion, which is not available for them (validation of the reconstruction: Supplementary S18). The datasets are public, de-identified benchmarks used under their providers' terms (Supplementary S16); no new human data were collected, and institutional review board approval was not required.

\subsection*{Decoder-only reference and in-network transfer}
The segmentation decoder $D_s$ produces a feature map $F_s\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times H\times W}$ with $c=32$ channels, and a $1\times1$ convolution $W_o$ gives logits $z_s$ for $K$ classes. The segmentation loss $L_{\mathrm{seg}}=L_{\mathrm{ce}}+L_{\mathrm{dice}}$ is pixel-averaged cross-entropy plus soft Dice computed per foreground class over the batch and averaged over classes, so that each class contributes its own ratio regardless of its area. This compound loss is a common default and was kept fixed; losses that reweight small or hard structures, such as the focal Tversky loss \citep{Abraham2019Focal}, were not used, so that every arm shares one segmentation loss. During training, an auxiliary boundary decoder $D_b$ with three upsampling blocks reads the encoder features and outputs a boundary feature $q_b\in\mathbb{R}^{c_b\times H\times W}$ ($c_b=16$) and a single-channel boundary logit map $z_b$. Its target $y_b$ is the union over foreground classes of the exclusive-or of binary dilation and erosion with a four-connected cross (one iteration), and $L_{\mathrm{boundary}}=\mathrm{BCE}(z_b,y_b)+L_{\mathrm{Dice}}(\sigma(z_b),y_b)$ with weight $\lambda_b=0.05$. D is trained with $L_{\mathrm{seg}}+\lambda_bL_{\mathrm{boundary}}$; the boundary gradient reaches the shared encoder, and $D_b$ is removed after training.

\emph{Projection and gate (G).} A $1\times1$ convolution $P$ maps the segmentation feature to $q_s=P(F_s)$ with the shape of $q_b$, and $q_s$ drives a gate:
\begin{equation}
g=\sigma\!\left(W_g q_s+b_g\right)\in(0,1)^{c\times H\times W},\qquad
F_s^{\mathrm{m}}=F_s\odot\bigl[1+\alpha\,(g-0.5)\bigr],\qquad z_s=W_oF_s^{\mathrm{m}},
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $W_g\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times c_b}$ is a $1\times1$ convolution, so the gate acts per pixel and per channel. Because $g\in(0,1)$, the factor lies in $[1-\alpha/2,\,1+\alpha/2]$: $\alpha$ bounds how much the gate can rescale a feature, and $\alpha=0$ makes the gate the identity. We set $\alpha=0.25$ ($\pm12.5\%$) in single-seed Synapse validation runs during development, in which $\alpha=0.5$ worsened HD95 for several organs, and froze it before the matched experiments, without test data (sensitivity: Supplementary S11).

\emph{Feature distillation (F).}
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{distill}}=\mathrm{SmoothL1}_{\beta=1}\bigl(q_s,\sg(q_b)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where the stop-gradient operator $\sg(\cdot)$ is the identity in the forward pass and has zero Jacobian in the backward pass (\texttt{detach()} in PyTorch). $L_{\mathrm{distill}}$ therefore updates $P$ and, through it, the segmentation decoder and encoder, but sends no gradient into $D_b$, which is trained only by $L_{\mathrm{boundary}}$. Because student and teacher features are joined by the learned map $P$, as in hint training \citep{Romero2015FitNets}, $L_{\mathrm{distill}}$ can decrease when $P$ reads information that $F_s$ already holds; Supplementary S13 therefore measures $q_s$ and $F_s$ separately.

\emph{Surface loss (S).} $L_{\mathrm{sdf}}$ is the boundary loss of \citet{Kervadec2019Boundary} with each image--class signed distance map (negative inside the object) divided by its largest absolute value, a fixed weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{sdf}}=1$ and no schedule (Supplementary S2). The objective of BDS-Lite is
\begin{equation}
L=L_{\mathrm{seg}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sdf}}L_{\mathrm{sdf}}+\lambda_bL_{\mathrm{boundary}}+\lambda_dL_{\mathrm{distill}},\qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{sdf}}=1,\ \lambda_b=\lambda_d=0.05,
\label{eq:bds}
\end{equation}
with weights set in the same development runs, frozen before the matched experiments and identical across datasets; each ablation arm drops terms or sets $\alpha=0$.

\subsection*{External-teacher distillation, controls and augmentation}
D+KD-band keeps D and its losses and adds a loss from a separate teacher. The teacher is a U-Net with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 encoder (segmentation\_models\_pytorch 0.6.0; \R{R3.teacher.m}\,M parameters on ACDC, about \R{R3.teacher.ratio} times the student). We chose it as a widely used larger model that differs from the student in capacity and pretraining. One teacher per dataset was trained once (seed 100) on the students' training images with their checkpoint rule, had to reach the validation DSC and Boundary F1 of D, and was then frozen; its class probabilities $p^{t}$ for every training image were cached. With $B_n$ the union over foreground classes of the dilation minus the erosion of the teacher's argmax mask with one $3\times3$ square operation,
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{kd}}=\frac{\sum_{n,u}B_n(u)\bigl(-\sum_k p^{t}_{nk}(u)\log p_{nk}(u)\bigr)}{\sum_{n,u}B_n(u)+10^{-6}},\qquad L=L_{\mathrm{seg}}+\lambda_bL_{\mathrm{boundary}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{kd}}L_{\mathrm{kd}},
\label{eq:kd}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{kd}}=1$, no temperature, and both distributions clamped to $[10^{-6},1-10^{-6}]$. D+KD-band is a simplified instance of boundary-and-body distillation \citep{Liu2024BPKD} that keeps only the band term. Method, teacher, weight and band rule were fixed before any run of this design and were not tuned.

\emph{Controls.} Each control removes one property that could be credited for a gain. D+KD-full removes the band restriction: it uses the same teacher, cache, weight and clamping with $B_n\equiv1$, so $L_{\mathrm{kd}}$ averages the soft cross-entropy over every pixel, background and empty slices included. The pixels near the teacher's contours remain in this loss, so the control keeps the teacher's boundary information and asks whether confining distillation to the band matters. D+KD-band$_{\mathrm{cmp}}$ removes the teacher's advantage in capacity and pretraining: it keeps the band rule, loss and weight of D+KD-band but uses a compact teacher, a network of the student's own architecture without pretraining, trained once by the D recipe (seed 100), frozen, and cached in the same way; it was not required to pass the external teacher's quality gate.

\emph{Augmentation comparison.} D$_{\mathrm{aug}}$ and D+KD-band$_{\mathrm{aug}}$ repeat D and D+KD-band with one transform per training sample, drawn uniformly from the eight elements of the dihedral group D4 (rotations by multiples of $90^{\circ}$, with or without a flip) and applied identically to the image, the mask, the boundary target and the cached teacher probabilities; test images are not transformed.

\subsection*{Training and evaluation}
All runs used AdamW (weight decay $10^{-5}$), cosine annealing over 150 epochs, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 and mixed precision, with batch size 8 and learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ on ISIC2018 and batch size 16 and learning rate $10^{-4}$ on ACDC and Synapse. The main protocol uses no data augmentation. All arms of a comparison then see the same input distribution, and the boundary signal is the only difference between them; the augmentation comparison tests whether the conclusions depend on this training regime. Checkpoints were selected on validation data, by the first maximum of a DSC that averages over slices or images and scores a class absent from both prediction and reference as 1; the reported analysis excludes such pairs and averages within patients or cases. Experiments ran on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Laptop GPU with Python 3.14.4, PyTorch 2.11.0, CUDA 13.0, cuDNN 9.19 and NumPy 2.4.4, as recorded with each run.

\emph{Metrics.} Predictions are the argmax over classes, with no thresholding, test-time augmentation or post-processing. Per image or slice and foreground class we compute DSC, IoU, Boundary F1, HD95 and ASSD and average over classes. Boundary F1 compares boundary maps obtained as the exclusive-or of dilation and erosion with a four-connected cross; a boundary pixel is matched if it lies within two cross-dilation steps (a Manhattan distance of 2\,px) of the other boundary. HD95 and ASSD are the 95th percentile and the mean of bidirectional surface distances on the $224\times224$ grid, in pixels. A class absent from both prediction and reference is excluded. A missed structure or false alarm scores approximately zero on DSC, IoU and Boundary F1 and has undefined distances, which are excluded from HD95 and ASSD; Supplementary S8 reports missed-structure and false-alarm rates and an all-case analysis that assigns the image diagonal to these pairs. These near-zero scores make per-slice scores much lower than volume-level scores of the same predictions (Tables~\ref{tab:errors} and~\ref{tab:published}).

Boundary F1 is the primary endpoint because it measures the property that boundary signals target, agreement of the contour within a tolerance, and is defined for every pair with a non-empty mask. It depends on the tolerance, ignores how far an unmatched boundary lies from the reference, and is computed on the resized grid. DSC and IoU (overlap) and HD95 and ASSD (distance, which do register distant errors) are therefore reported as secondary, descriptive metrics.

\subsection*{Statistics}
The unit of replication is the training run; image-level tests of a fixed model describe that model and often separate runs of the same recipe (Supplementary S14). For run $r$ of arm $a$, $y_{aru}$ is the class-averaged score averaged over the samples of test unit $u$ (an image on ISIC2018, a patient on ACDC, a case on Synapse), and $m_{ar}$ is its mean over the $U$ units defined in every run of both arms. With $n_a$ runs per arm,
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\bar m_A-\bar m_B,\qquad
\mathrm{SE}^2=\underbrace{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}+\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}_{\text{between runs}}+\underbrace{\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_u(d_u)}{U}}_{\text{test units}},\qquad
d_u=\bar y_{A\cdot u}-\bar y_{B\cdot u},
\label{eq:se}
\end{equation}
where $s_a^2$ is the sample variance of the run means of arm $a$ and $\bar y_{a\cdot u}$ averages unit $u$ over the runs of arm $a$. $\Delta$ is oriented so that positive favours the first arm (signs reversed for HD95 and ASSD). Intervals are $\Delta\pm t_{0.975,\nu}\,\mathrm{SE}$, with Welch--Satterthwaite degrees of freedom $\nu$ computed from the between-run terms. Both terms of Eq.~\ref{eq:se} contain the residual variance between runs and test units, so under an additive model the estimator counts that variance twice and the intervals are somewhat conservative (Supplementary S20). Contrasts between ablation cells of the three-run study use the within-cell variance of run means pooled over the cells of the contrast, and are combined over the three datasets by inverse variance as fixed-effect summaries with normal intervals. Supplementary S24 examines the sensitivity of the pooled summaries to the pooling rule.

We classify each contrast as confirmed, detected or not detected. A single contrast is \emph{detected} if its 95\% interval excludes zero. In a family of contrasts tested together, detection requires a Holm-adjusted $p$ \citep{Holm1979A} below 0.05; the families are the three datasets of the first external-teacher study and the four control contrasts. The pooled contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:innetwork}A and the ten-run ISIC2018 contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:innetwork}B are labelled detected from their unadjusted intervals; for the latter, Holm correction over the six contrasts is applied to the fresh seeds 4--10, and a contrast is confirmed only if it passes that test. An effect is \emph{confirmed} if it was detected under a decision rule fixed before the deciding data were used. A result that is \emph{not detected} does not show that the effect is absent. Table~\ref{tab:studies} gives the evidence level of each study. A difference of 0.01 in Boundary F1 was preset as the smallest effect of interest and is drawn as a band in the figures; it is an analysis threshold without clinical validation, and the readings against it are given in the supplement. Two analyses were added after all results were known and are marked post hoc: the augmentation-by-distillation interaction, which is the difference between the two distillation contrasts on the same patients (Eq.~\ref{eq:se} extended to four arms; Supplementary S24), and the scores of the frozen teachers (Supplementary S23).

\emph{Feature distillation and gate.} Added to D, they changed pooled Boundary F1 by \R{IN.nosurf.pooled.boundary_f1.est} (95\% CI \R{IN.nosurf.pooled.boundary_f1.text}) without the surface loss and by \R{IN.surf.pooled.boundary_f1.est} (\R{IN.surf.pooled.boundary_f1.text}) with it, and no gain was detected on any dataset (Table~\ref{tab:innetwork}A). \section*{Discussion}

\subsection*{When does boundary distillation help?}
In the compact U-Net studied here, boundary distillation helped when it came from an external teacher and training used no augmentation; this gain was confirmed on ACDC with \R{CF.n} runs per arm, under the prespecified rule as a gain of unclear size (Supplementary S18). Distillation inside the network added no detectable gain to the boundary decoder in an ablation with three runs per arm, which could not exclude gains of up to about 0.02. For distillation from a frozen teacher, the controls point to a general effect on training more than to a transfer of boundary knowledge. These effects are of the order of the standard deviation between retrainings of one recipe, so a single training per arm could not have separated them from chance.

\subsection*{What the diagnostics show about the complete BDS-Lite recipe}
The complete recipe adds three components to D, and the diagnostics of the trained networks suggest that none of them supplied boundary information that the decoder had not already supplied. Feature distillation did what its loss asks: a boundary head applied to the projected feature $q_s$ found boundaries far better in distilled than in undistilled arms (average precision \R{MECH.isic2018.head.dist} against \R{MECH.isic2018.head.plain} on ISIC2018). The feature that makes the prediction was unchanged: a linear boundary readout from $F_s$ differed by \R{MECH.isic2018.fs.diff} on ISIC2018 between distilled and undistilled arms, and the interval includes zero on each of the three datasets (Supplementary S13). Hint training permits exactly this \citep{Romero2015FitNets}: the learned projection can satisfy the loss by reading boundary information that $F_s$ already holds through the shared encoder. The gate, in turn, learned a nearly constant per-channel scale, so that replacing it by the identity left Boundary F1 almost unchanged (Inference cost). Gradient probes gave no sign of a conflict between the objectives on the shared encoder (Supplementary S10).

The recipe thus pays for three additional components without a measurable gain in the predicting feature. With three runs per arm, every ISIC2018 interval of Table~\ref{tab:innetwork}A includes zero, so the factorial contrasts cannot assign the ISIC2018 deficit to one component. Two observations make the surface term the first candidate: U-Net+GSL, the other recipe with a distance-weighted surface term, also scored below D on ISIC2018 (a deficit of unclear size), and in an exploratory additive model the surface loss had a negative ISIC2018 coefficient ($-$0.024, 95\% CI $-$0.040 to $-$0.009; Supplementary S13).

\subsection*{What the external teacher transfers}
Output distillation \citep{Hinton2015Distilling} restricted to a boundary band, as in the boundary term of boundary-and-body distillation \citep{Liu2024BPKD}, raised Boundary F1 over the reference in the replication. In the exploratory controls, a gain remained without the band restriction and without the stronger teacher: whole-image distillation from the same teacher raised Boundary F1, and so did band distillation from a compact teacher that scored below the reference it was distilled into. Gains from a teacher no larger than its student are known from born-again networks \citep{Furlanello2018Born}, and soft targets from a weak teacher have been analysed as learned label smoothing \citep{Yuan2020Revisiting}. Our results are consistent with such a regularizing effect. They do not exclude a contribution of the band or of the pretrained teacher: neither difference was detected, but their point estimates, \R{FU.C1.boundary_f1.delta} and \R{FU.C4.boundary_f1.delta}, amount to roughly two fifths and one third of the replicated gain of \R{FIN.cf.abs}.

One mechanism fits these observations. The teacher's probabilities were cached on its own training images, which are also the students' training images. Away from the contours, a teacher fitted to these images is likely to predict close to the reference labels, so the distillation target departs from the hard labels mainly near the contours, where it is soft. Distillation then acts like label smoothing concentrated near the contours, whether the loss covers the band or the whole image, and a compact teacher fitted to the same images supplies a target of the same kind. We offer this as an interpretation; we did not measure how far the cached probabilities depart from the reference labels.

With augmentation in both arms, a distillation gain was not detected, and in a post hoc test the distillation contrast was smaller with augmentation than without; augmentation may fill part of the same regularizing role. The teacher itself was trained without augmentation, and its lead over the reference shrank from \R{TE.ext.D.delta} to \R{TE.ext.Daug.delta} in Boundary F1 when the reference was trained with augmentation (post hoc; Supplementary S23), so this comparison cannot separate a weaker effect of distillation under augmentation from a smaller margin between teacher and student.

\subsection*{Implications for practice}
These implications concern contour agreement on public benchmarks; the study did not measure a clinical outcome. On ACDC, augmentation changed Boundary F1 several times more than distillation did (exploratory, not prespecified), so for developers of compact segmentation networks the augmentation regime should be settled first and any boundary signal judged under it. Output distillation from a teacher is worth trying when augmentation cannot be used; in our exploratory controls on ACDC, a gain remained without the band restriction and with a compact teacher. In a three-run ablation, we found no support for adding the in-network transfer of BDS-Lite to a boundary decoder. The decoder itself adds nothing at inference and is inexpensive to try.

For studies of such methods, the training run should be the unit of replication, and the standard deviations reported here can serve as a noise baseline when planning the number of runs on these datasets. Controls should remove the property credited for a gain: the same network with its own boundary supervision and no transfer, the same loss over the whole image, and a teacher of the student's size.

\subsection*{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \emph{Scope.} The results concern one compact 2D U-Net trained without pretraining, three public datasets and two augmentation regimes, with slices scored independently and distances in pixels of the $224\times224$ grid. The main protocol used no augmentation, so the references are weak in absolute terms (Table~\ref{tab:published}) and the confirmed gain (Supplementary S18) was obtained without augmentation; in the five studies no plain U-Net was trained with augmentation (a development run was scored on validation images only; Supplementary S15), so the study cannot say what a boundary signal adds to a strong, well-regularized model or whether the decoder gain persists under augmentation. The results do not extend to larger, transformer-based or 3D models, to other tasks such as tumour segmentation, or to physical distances and clinical indices.
\item \emph{Precision of the in-network results.} The ablation used three runs per arm, and Table~\ref{tab:innetwork}A adds feature distillation and the gate as a pair; cells that add them one at a time exist only together with the surface loss (D+G+S and D+F+S; Supplementary S7, S13). Its pooled estimates are dominated by ACDC and depend on the pooling rule and on the variance estimator (Supplementary S20, S24). Three-run checkpoints of an earlier training pipeline, whose script is not preserved, did not show the decoder gain, and their interval for the ISIC2018 deficit of BDS-Lite is too wide to confirm or contradict it; at a 4\,px tolerance the decoder gain on ISIC2018 was not detected (Supplementary S15). The diagnostics of the trained networks are observations and establish no cause.
\item \emph{The replication and its controls.} The confirmed gain, of unclear size under the prespecified rule (Supplementary S18), covers one dataset, ACDC, chosen after the first external-teacher study; ISIC2018 and Synapse have only three-run estimates. The official patients come from the training centre, and their preprocessing is our reconstruction of an unavailable conversion (Supplementary S18). The controls, the augmentation comparison and the interaction test reuse these patients after their results were known. Each teacher was trained once and without augmentation, and the two teachers differ in several properties at once. The regularization reading was not tested against a soft-label control, and the external-teacher and in-network results come from different patients, seeds and run counts, so their sizes are not compared.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Conclusions}
For a compact 2D U-Net, in-network boundary distillation added no detectable gain beyond a training-only boundary decoder in a three-run ablation on three public datasets. Distillation from an external teacher raised Boundary F1 on held-out ACDC patients in a \R{CF.n}-run replication when training used no augmentation (under the prespecified rule, a gain of unclear size), in a pattern consistent with regularization. On ACDC, flip-and-rotation augmentation changed Boundary F1 several times more than distillation did (exploratory, not prespecified), and with it a distillation gain was not detected. Because these effects are as large as the variation between retrainings, and because augmentation outweighed distillation on ACDC, boundary signals should be judged under the intended augmentation regime and over repeated training runs. Future work should compare distillation with soft labels derived from the reference masks, train teachers and decoders with augmentation, and repeat the controls on further datasets and with stronger and 3D models.
